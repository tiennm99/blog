## [Special Cases in Go](https://www.dolthub.com/blog/2026-05-22-special-cases-in-go/)

Go is frequently marketed as a "simple" language that deliberately avoids special cases. Nick Tobey points out that Go actually has plenty of exceptions hiding in unexpected places. The `init` function is the clearest example: it runs automatically when a package is imported, takes no arguments, returns no values, can be declared multiple times within the same package, yet cannot be called manually. If you try to call it, the compiler reports `undefined: init` without further explanation. Similarly, the `internal` directory creates a visibility scope narrower than public/private, yet that rule doesn't exist in the Go language specification at all—it is enforced by the `go` toolchain.

The reason is that the language spec intentionally leaves import path interpretation implementation-defined. That flexibility lets build systems like Bazel apply their own rules while allowing the standard `go` toolchain to evolve without amending the language spec—adding special cases like `internal` directories or `_test` package suffixes that live in the same directory as the package under test. Tobey also notes that a package's declared name doesn't have to match its directory name, forcing IDEs to parse every imported package just to resolve where an identifier comes from; for new code, explicit import aliases avoid the ambiguity. The broader takeaway: while most of these quirks rarely get in the way of day-to-day work, they show that a language's simplicity often just means the complexity was pushed somewhere else.

## [Writing Code vs. Shipping Code: Productivity Effects Across Generations of AI Coding Tools](https://muratbuffalo.blogspot.com/2026/06/writing-code-vs-shipping-code.html)

Murat Demirbas analyzes an MIT and Wharton study that combines internal Microsoft telemetry with public GitHub traces from over 100,000 developers to measure the real-world productivity impact of AI coding tools. The study divides tools into three generations: autocomplete, synchronous agents like Claude Code or Cursor, and asynchronous agents. Output gains at the lines-of-code level are striking, but attenuate sharply at each downstream stage—from files to commits, pull requests, repositories, and finally shipped releases: with autocomplete, lines of code jump by over 228% while shipped releases rise by only ~10%; with synchronous agents, the corresponding numbers are 741% and 20%.

While Demirbas is skeptical of applying a linear manufacturing production function to software development, he finds the framing useful because it exposes human coordination and review costs. Translating the findings into Amdahl's Law, he calculates that the parallelizable, acceleratable fraction of software work sits right around 35% across all three tool generations—matching the share of time developers actually spend writing code. Agents produce more shipped releases not because they expand that 35% slice, but because they accelerate it more aggressively and intervene closer to the finish line. The remaining 65%—understanding the problem, planning, team alignment, and code review—remains a serial bottleneck. To genuinely ship faster, organizations have to improve those non-coding stages rather than just generating code faster.

## [When Was the Last Time You Did Just One Thing?](https://alifeengineered.substack.com/p/when-was-the-last-time-you-did-just)

Steve Huynh recounts packing for a long trip: downloading dozens of YouTube videos, queueing up a backlog of podcasts, packing a handheld game console, two physical books, a Kindle, and his laptop just in case he wanted to get some work done. Then it hit him that this entire "arsenal" against a single moment of boredom would cause him to miss the trip itself—and that paradoxically, the harder we try not to waste a single second, the emptier every second feels. While the public preview ends there, the message from the title and setup is unmistakable: doing just one thing at a time is no longer our default state, but a skill we have to actively practice.

For people in tech, it is a timely reminder about attention management. Not every quiet gap in the day needs to be filled with upskilling, podcasts, or side work. When every spare moment is stuffed with fresh stimulation, we gradually lose the ability to observe, rest properly, think deeply, and be fully present with whatever is in front of us. Looking busy and optimized isn't the same as living intentionally; leaving blank space in your day is part of sustainable productivity, especially in engineering roles already fragmented by constant context switching.

## [Predicting AI job exposure](https://www.ben-evans.com/benedictevans/2026/5/24/ai-job-exposure)

Benedict Evans critiques efforts to score which professions, companies, or industries are most "exposed" to AI disruption. In his view, this amounts to predicting the unpredictable, and the simplest sanity check is running the same methodology backward against previous technology waves. Over the past century, we automated accounting with mainframes, databases, spreadsheets, and ERPs—yet the total number of accountants kept growing. When analysis becomes cheap and fast, organizations do far more of it and ask different questions, while new regulations create entire categories of compliance work.

A second blind spot is that a job's core tasks can stay unchanged while the business model underneath it collapses: the internet didn't automate away the craft of journalism, it destroyed the classified-ad monopoly that funded newsrooms. Third are second-order shocks nobody extrapolates from task lists, like smartphone GPS enabling Uber and reshaping the taxi industry. Deeper still, occupational databases like O*NET cannot capture what a real job actually entails, much like early expert systems failed to capture tacit human expertise. Evans concludes that while high-level frameworks are useful for thinking, granular job-by-job forecasts at this early stage will only be right by accident, because we have no way of knowing whether the exceptions will dwarf the rule.

## [Choosing Values for Robust Tests](https://testing.googleblog.com/2026/06/choosing-values-for-robust-tests.html)

Radion Khait highlights a subtle testing bug that is remarkably easy to miss: a test passes not because the implementation is correct, but because the test input happens to match the type's default value. In the post's example, a custom map's `insert` method forgets to assign the `value` parameter and simply default-initializes the entry. A test that inserts `0` and asserts that lookup returns `0` passes with flying colors, because `0` is already the default value for an integer. The result is a false sense of security: the method was called, but nothing proved the input was actually stored.

From that example, Khait distills three practical rules for choosing test inputs. First, always use non-default values: non-zero numbers, non-empty strings, and enum variants other than the first declared value—changing `0` to `5` exposes the map bug immediately. Second, where appropriate, test representative inputs across equivalence classes such as empty, missing, `null`, boundary values, and inputs that trigger complex branches, optionally pairing with fuzzing for broader coverage. Third, use distinct values for every parameter so tests immediately catch implementations that accidentally reuse an argument or swap parameter order; parameterized tests make it easy to cover multiple input combinations without duplicating test code.

## [AI demands more engineering discipline. Not less](https://charity.wtf/p/ai-demands-more-engineering-discipline)

Charity Majors pushes back against a growing temptation: if AI generates code quickly and reasonably well, can we relax code reviews, verification, and engineering rigor? Her answer is the exact opposite. As the cost of generating code plummets, lines of code stop being a precious asset and become a disposable artifact of our current understanding. A software team's true output is shared understanding, and what retains lasting value is everything that tells us whether the system is actually working: requirements, invariants, failure modes, tests, observability, and reproducibility.

She compares the AI wave to the shift from hand-tended "pet" servers to immutable infrastructure. Once servers could be torn down and rebuilt at will, engineering discipline didn't vanish—it moved up a level into configuration management, provisioning pipelines, verification, and repeatable operations. Software is undergoing the same shift: when code can be regenerated cheaply, individual lines of code stop being the highest-leverage place to spend review energy, and focus moves to artifacts that specify behavior, architecture, trade-offs, and production outcomes. Human brains were never great at statically verifying code anyway, and non-deterministic AI systems make tracing, testing in production, record-and-replay, and tight feedback loops even more critical. Far from making software less rigorous, AI demands more engineering discipline than ever.

## [Nine Questions I Now Ask in Interviews That I Wish I'd Asked Five Years Ago](https://louisedeason.substack.com/p/nine-questions-i-now-ask-in-interviews)

Louise Deason reminds candidates that an interview is a two-way evaluation, even though most people still treat it like a school exam—which conveniently benefits the employer. The "do you have any questions for us?" segment at the end is the most underutilized part of the process, precisely because interviewers let their guard down and inadvertently reveal how the org actually runs. She shares nine questions designed to force specific, revealing answers: how the most recent promotion on the team happened, what the longest-tenured person in this role is doing now, what the team is genuinely bad at, how the team handled a recent disagreement with leadership, headcount plans for the next year, whether you can meet the people you'll actually work with day to day, what six-month success and failure look like, when the last reorg happened, and why this role is open.

The real signal lies in how the interviewer responds: whether the answer is concrete, evasive, or hesitant. A clear story about promotion criteria or an honest admission of a team's weak spots signals transparency; conversely, claims that "we always agree," reluctance to let you speak with future peers, or an awkward pause when asked why the seat is open are major red flags. Her closing reminder: you are always choosing between differently imperfect options, and any company that gets defensive when asked thoughtful questions doesn't deserve your time.

## [Agentic Testing: Where Agents Fit in the E2E Testing Stack](https://slack.engineering/agentic-testing-where-agents-fit-in-the-e2e-testing-stack/)

Sergii Gorbachov at Slack shares findings from over 200 end-to-end test runs powered by AI agents, exploring where agentic testing belongs in the testing stack. Traditional E2E tests enforce a rigid, scripted path through the UI, whereas agentic tests start from a high-level goal: the agent observes the current UI state, decides the next action on the fly, and verifies the outcome. The team benchmarked three approaches—an agent driving Playwright MCP, an agent driving Playwright CLI, and AI-generated deterministic Playwright test scripts—against a staging workspace with synthetic data across two user flows: replying to a thread and search discovery.

The AI-generated Playwright scripts ran fastest at around 3 minutes, but their failure rate spiked to ~48% on the more complex flow. Using Sonnet 4.5 to run the agent and Opus 4.6 to generate tests, Playwright MCP achieved the lowest failure rate at 0–12%, compared to 12–20% for Playwright CLI, where most failures stemmed from login and navigation hiccups at the execution layer. MCP also required fewer interaction turns because it batches actions and returns updated state in a single round-trip. However, each agentic test run cost $15–$30 and took over 5 minutes, with most of the cost driven by repeatedly re-sending conversation history. Slack's conclusion: deterministic E2E tests remain the backbone for fast CI regression checks, while agentic testing sits as a complementary layer at the top of the testing pyramid—ideal for exploratory testing of complex flows, diagnosing flaky journeys, and reproducing production bugs.

## [Making Agents Easy: 13 Lessons from Forter's Agentic AI Sprint](https://blog.forter.dev/making-agents-easy-13-lessons-from-forters-agentic-ai-sprint/)

Ben Maraney recounts how Forter ran a two-week company-wide sprint to get their entire R&D organization building AI agents. The core thesis isn't that building agents is inherently effortless, but that an organization can systematically remove friction across three layers: tooling, agent runtime infrastructure, and organizational guardrails. On the tooling side, Forter built an internal MCP server called Toolchain: engineers open a web UI, pick and configure the tools they need, and immediately get an API key; adding a new internal tool takes just a declarative YAML file and a thin wrapper. By the end of the two-week sprint, Toolchain had grown to over 90 internal tools. And rather than standing up a complex custom RAG pipeline, they exposed their existing enterprise search engine as an MCP tool.

The post shares several pragmatic operational lessons as well. Agent builders need full visibility into every raw tool request and response—one Forter incident-response agent appeared to "solve" an outage brilliantly until traces revealed it had simply read the already-written postmortem doc. For internal human-in-the-loop tools, teams don't need formal eval suites on day one; prioritize tracing and observability first. Bring legal and security in early, enforce permissions in deterministic code rather than prompts, and put token costs on a visible dashboard. Finally, when training teams, tell them to treat an LLM like a bright new-grad intern: broad textbook knowledge, zero institutional context—meaning the responsibility for giving clear instructions rests squarely on the builder.

## [Agentic Code Review](https://addyosmani.com/blog/agentic-code-review/)

Addy Osmani argues that as coding agents write code faster and faster, the primary bottleneck in software engineering shifts from writing code to verifying and trusting it. AI can produce mountains of syntactically valid, test-accompanied, plausible-looking code, but human reading comprehension hasn't sped up to match. That makes code review the highest-leverage skill in modern engineering: not merely hunting for typos, but reconstructing intent, assessing blast radius, spreading system knowledge across the team, and deciding whether a change should exist at all. Industry data from 2026 cited in the post shows that while code output has surged, code churn, defects, production incidents, and review wait times have climbed right alongside it.

There is no single review policy that fits every context. Solo side projects with no users can lean heavily on automated tests and AI review; long-lived production systems handling real users, money, or sensitive data require risk-tiered human review. Osmani recommends keeping pull requests small, stating intent explicitly, requiring concrete proof of verification (such as real test outputs) before human review begins, reading test diffs more closely than implementation diffs, treating deterministic CI as a non-negotiable guardrail, and using AI reviewers as sensors rather than final judges. Humans may no longer read every single generated line, but they still own the merge button, carry accountability for production, and must focus their deepest scrutiny on high-blast-radius changes like auth, billing, security, and privacy.

## [I Thought Redis Was Just a HashMap](https://mukul0x9.pages.dev/blog/memdb/)

Mukul Makwana starts from a deceptively simple question: how different is Redis or Memcached from a global hash map supporting `set`, `get`, and `del`? To find out, he built a minimal in-memory database from scratch in Go. Version one wrapped Go's built-in `map`, accepted commands over TCP, and spawned one goroutine per client connection. The bottleneck appeared immediately as soon as multiple goroutines read and wrote concurrently: the map needed synchronization, and a single global mutex forced every operation to wait in line.

To fix that, Makwana replaced the built-in map with a two-array arena design: `bucket_array` stores offsets while `data_array` packs metadata, keys, values, and next-entry offsets for collision chaining in contiguous byte slices. To reduce lock contention, the keyspace is partitioned into shards, each with its own arrays and `RWMutex`, while a background goroutine handles shard resizing when load factors climb. Because `data_array` is append-only, deletes simply mark a tombstone bit, and a compaction pass rebuilds the shard once dead bytes cross a threshold—at the cost of locking that shard during compaction. Benchmarking `SET` workloads showed Go's built-in `map` still winning on raw throughput and stop-the-world pause times, but the custom pointer-free arena slashed concurrent GC mark time from ~113 ms down to 7.8 ms. Even then, this toy engine still lacks persistence, eviction policies, crash recovery, and replication—the exact things that make real Redis a serious piece of engineering.
