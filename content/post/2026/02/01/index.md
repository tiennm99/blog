## [The Next Two Years of Software Engineering](https://addyosmani.com/blog/next-two-years/)

Addy Osmani observes that the software industry is at an inflection point: AI coding tools have evolved from autocomplete suggestions into autonomous agents that execute end-to-end tasks, while the hiring boom has given way to an efficiency mandate where companies favor experienced engineers and small, well-tooled teams. Rather than making single-track predictions, he poses five open questions through 2026, each paired with two contrasting scenarios. Junior hiring could collapse (a Harvard study found junior roles dropped roughly 9–10% after companies adopted generative AI) or rebound as software expands into every industry. Foundational computer science skills could atrophy or become more valuable than ever. The developer role could narrow into an AI code reviewer or expand into a full-system orchestrator. Narrow specialists risk displacement, while T-shaped engineers (deep in one or two areas, broad across many) are in high demand. And traditional CS degrees could be overtaken by bootcamps, online courses, and internal apprenticeships.

With 84% of developers already using or planning to use AI, value lies in reviewing AI output for logic bugs, security vulnerabilities, and requirement drift; the best engineers aren't the ones who write code the fastest, but the ones who know when not to trust AI. Juniors should use AI to learn rather than as a crutch; seniors should anchor quality assurance, mentorship, and architecture. The common denominator is continuous learning.

## [Databases in 2025: A Year in Review](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~pavlo/blog/2026/01/2025-databases-retrospective.html)

Andy Pavlo (Carnegie Mellon University) looks back at the database world in 2025, starting with PostgreSQL's continued dominance. Version 18 added an asynchronous I/O subsystem and B-tree skip scans. The business side was even livelier: Databricks paid $1 billion to acquire Neon, Snowflake paid $250 million for CrunchyData, Microsoft launched HorizonDB, and three horizontally scalable PostgreSQL sharding projects—Multigres (Supabase), Neki (PlanetScale), and PgDog—entered the ring. 2025 was also the year every DBMS shipped an MCP (Model Context Protocol) server, exploding after OpenAI backed the standard in March. Pavlo cautions that these servers mostly just forward queries to the database, making least-privilege access essential. The columnar file format space heated up as well, with five new formats challenging Parquet: FastLanes, F3, Vortex, AnyBlox, and Amudai.

The industry roundup covers a wave of acquisitions: IBM bought DataStax (for an estimated $3 billion) and Confluent, Salesforce acquired Informatica ($8 billion), and Fivetran and dbt Labs announced a surprise merger. Databricks raised two massive funding rounds of $4 billion and $1 billion, while several database startups shut down, including Fauna, PostgresML, Hydra, MyScaleDB, and Voltron Data. The post closes with Oracle founder Larry Ellison briefly becoming the richest person in the world with an estimated net worth of $393 billion—which Pavlo notes makes him the richest database person in history, surpassing even John D. Rockefeller on an inflation-adjusted basis.

For the first time, enterprises will spend more on agents than on human labor in certain workflows, much as riders willingly pay a ~31% premium to take a Waymo over an Uber. 2026 is poised to set a liquidity record with potential IPOs from SpaceX, OpenAI, Anthropic, Stripe, and Databricks. ## [The Garbage Collection Handbook](https://gchandbook.org/index.html)

This page introduces the second edition of *The Garbage Collection Handbook*, the classic reference on automatic memory management. Descending from Richard Jones's original *Garbage Collection* (Wiley, 1996) and the first Handbook edition in 2012, this new edition thoroughly updates the material to reflect modern hardware, software, and runtime environments. It progresses from simple, traditional algorithms to modern parallel, incremental, concurrent, and real-time collectors, illustrated throughout with pseudocode and diagrams. The book analyzes high-performance commercial collectors in detail, explains tricky runtime interface mechanics, and adds over 90 pages of new material including chapters on persistence (non-volatile memory) and energy-aware garbage collection.

Because nearly all modern programming languages rely on garbage collection, understanding how collectors work helps developers confidently select and tune them for their workloads. The e-book edition features over 37,000 internal cross-references linking chapters, sections, algorithms, figures, and original research papers, accompanied by an online bibliography database of nearly 3,400 garbage-collection publications. The first edition of the Handbook was also translated into Chinese and Japanese in 2016.

## [Vibe-Coded Is the New "Made in China"](https://gabriel-afonso.com/blog/vibe-coded-is-the-new-made-in-china/)

Gabriel Afonso notices that on `r/selfhosted`, any new project labeled "vibe-coded" is immediately met with skepticism. Coined by Andrej Karpathy in early 2025, "vibe coding" means giving in to the vibes and letting large language models handle the implementation while you focus only on what you want to build. In Afonso's view, the backlash usually isn't about the code quality itself, but what the label signals. Historically, the sheer difficulty of building software acted as a natural filter: anyone who shipped an open-source project had invested enough sweat equity that they were likely to stick around and maintain it. Now that anyone can spin up an app in a few hours and code far beyond their actual skill level, a project's author might not even understand the code they published. As a result, "vibe-coded" has become like the old "Made in China" label—shorthand for cheap, disposable software.

He draws a parallel to chess: after Deep Blue defeated Kasparov in 1997, chess became even more popular as humans learned to work alongside engines. Respected developers like DHH, Tanner Linsley, and Boris Cherny (creator of Claude Code, who shared that 100% of his contributions over the prior 30 days were written by Claude Code) use AI heavily without drawing backlash because they remain true authors who understand and own their systems. Time remains the most reliable filter: a project maintained for two years with real users proves a level of commitment that cannot be faked. Today's skepticism is simply the ecosystem's immune system adapting while the rules are being rewritten.

## [The production bug that made me care about undefined behavior](https://gaultier.github.io/blog/the_production_bug_that_made_me_care_about_undefined_behavior.html)

Philippe Gaultier recounts a production bug in a C++ system processing billions of euros in payments each year. An HTTP endpoint was supposed to return either `error` or `succeeded` set to `true`—never both—yet clients occasionally received responses with both fields set to `true`, even though each field was assigned at most once in mutually exclusive branches. The culprit was the declaration `Response response;`. In C, reading an uninitialized struct field is straightforward undefined behavior; C++ is subtler: because `Response` contained a `std::string`, it was a non-POD type, so the compiler synthesized and called a default constructor—which default-constructed the `std::string` but left the two `bool` fields uninitialized with garbage stack bytes.

The fix is to use value initialization (`Response response{};`) to zero-initialize every field, or provide explicit default member initializers. Compilers emit no warning even with all warning flags enabled; `clang-tidy` catches it statically, and AddressSanitizer paired with UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer (or Valgrind) catches it at runtime, though only if test coverage hits that code path and the stack happens to contain non-zero bytes. Gaultier wrote a custom `libclang` plugin to scan the entire codebase for similar bugs. The post also highlights a bizarre C++ quirk: reading an uninitialized `std::byte` or `unsigned char` is permitted in many cases without triggering undefined behavior, whereas reading an uninitialized `bool` is instant UB. His takeaway: C++ has far too many initialization syntaxes, looks deceptively like C while behaving differently, and changes its rules across language standards.

## [Don't fall into the anti-AI hype](https://antirez.com/news/158)

Salvatore Sanfilippo (`antirez`), creator of Redis, loves crafting software line by line, yet admits that AI has changed programming forever. He used to think this inflection point was still a few years away, but changed his mind now that modern LLMs can complete major features or medium-sized projects almost autonomously. In a single week, by writing prompts and occasionally reviewing code to steer direction, he finished four projects in hours that would otherwise have taken weeks: adding UTF-8 support to his `linenoise` library along with a pseudo-terminal test harness; fixing transient timing and TCP deadlock test failures in Redis; having Claude Code write a 700-line pure C library in 5 minutes to run BERT-style embedding inference within 15% of PyTorch's speed; and having Claude Code reproduce internal Redis Streams changes from a design spec in about 20 minutes.

In his view, writing code by hand is no longer necessary for most projects. He is happy that his open-source code was used to train models, seeing it as a continuation of the open-source mission to democratize code and knowledge; AI will empower small teams to compete with tech giants just as open source did in the 1990s. Still, he worries about this technology concentrating in the hands of a few corporations. His advice: don't fall for anti-AI hype—give modern tools a serious multi-week trial rather than a five-minute test, because the joy of programming is building, and we can now build more and better than ever.

## [Performance Hints](https://abseil.io/fast/hints.html)

This is the "Performance Hints" guide written by Jeff Dean and Sanjay Ghemawat on Google's Abseil site, distilling the principles and techniques they use when optimizing software performance. They quote Donald Knuth in full: we should forget about small efficiencies about 97% of the time, yet we should not pass up our opportunities in that critical 3%. The common advice to "write it simply first and optimize later" often fails in practice because large systems built without regard for performance end up with flat profiles where CPU time is diffused across thousands of functions with no obvious hotspot; instead, engineers should instinctively choose the faster alternative whenever it doesn't noticeably harm readability. The guide shows how to do back-of-the-envelope estimates using basic latency numbers (L1 cache ~0.5 ns, branch mispredict ~5 ns, main memory ~50 ns), measure with profilers, and tackle flat CPU profiles by stacking many 1% wins or restructuring higher-level architecture.

The remainder of the document is a catalog of concrete techniques with code examples: designing APIs that allow optimization behind encapsulation boundaries (such as bulk operations and passing buffers); algorithmic improvements; compact memory representations that touch fewer cache lines (indices instead of pointers, arenas, arrays instead of maps, bitvectors); reducing allocations by pre-sizing containers, reusing objects, and avoiding copies; avoiding unnecessary work via fast paths, precomputation, caching, and keeping logging off hot paths; controlling code size; and parallelizing with short critical sections, sharding to reduce contention, SIMD, and avoiding false sharing. It concludes with specific advice for Protocol Buffers and containers like `absl::flat_hash_map`.

## [Software Engineering Job Market 2026: Data, Trends and Outlook](https://www.finalroundai.com/blog/software-engineering-job-market-2026)

This article analyzes why software engineering—once considered the safest career track—now feels vulnerable amid near-weekly layoff headlines. Indeed job-posting data via FRED shows software roles peaked in mid-2022, plunged through 2024, and have yet to rebound to pandemic highs. The primary driver isn't AI, but the 2021–2022 digitization bubble: fear of missing out and near-zero interest rates led everyone from Big Tech to startups to overhire massively. When demand normalized, AI became a convenient scapegoat for headcount reductions. Another major factor is global remote hiring: a senior engineer might cost $100,000+ per year in the US versus $40,000 in India, and telling investors "we're replacing roles with AI" sounds much more forward-looking than "we're offshoring." A METR study even found that experienced developers were 19% slower when using AI tools across 246 tasks.

Even so, the profession isn't disappearing—only demand for basic boilerplate coding is shrinking. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics still projects ~15% employment growth for software developers, SignalFire reports that Meta, Netflix, Uber, and Google are hiring faster than attrition, and Antler founder Magnus Grimeland points out that more AI-generated code means more bugs that require human engineers to fix. What has changed is the skill bar: system design, performance, security, and effective AI orchestration. Entry-level candidates face the toughest market, with junior postings down roughly 40% compared to pre-2022 levels. The article closes with recommended languages to learn in 2026: Python for AI/ML, JavaScript/TypeScript for frontend, Go for backend and cloud infrastructure, Java for enterprise, and Swift for iOS.
