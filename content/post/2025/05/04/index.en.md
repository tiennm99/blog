## [To fork or not to fork?](https://www.augmentcode.com/blog/to-fork-or-not-to-fork)

Scott Dietzen, CEO of Augment Code (and former CEO of Pure Storage), discusses a key architectural decision facing AI coding assistants: running as a plugin inside standard IDEs versus forking VS Code outright. GitHub Copilot and Augment chose the former, operating as plugins in VS Code, JetBrains, or Vim. Cursor and Windsurf (by Codeium) chose the latter, modifying the VS Code source directly for deeper AI integration.

According to Dietzen, forking yields short-term advantages, but users pay the price: they are forced out of their familiar IDEs (especially painful for JetBrains users), lose official Microsoft support, run into issues with official plugins like Python, C++, Docker, or Jupyter, lose access to the official VS Code extension marketplace, and have to wait for the fork maintainers to port upstream features. For the long-term benefit of customers, Augment chose the plugin route and focused on its core strength of supporting large, complex codebases. Dietzen also notes that Cursor is the third most popular IDE among users trialing Augment, showing that the two products can complement each other.

## [Why I'm No Longer Talking to Architects About Microservices](https://blog.container-solutions.com/why-im-no-longer-talking-to-architects-about-microservices)

Ian Miell explains why he no longer wants to debate microservices with software architects. The first problem is that nobody agrees on what "microservices" actually means: some measure it by lines of code, others tie it to team structure, and others equate it with container deployments. Like DevOps or Agile, the term has lost much of its original meaning through overuse.

The second problem is that discussions are typically abstract and disconnected from business goals: promises of scalability, agility, or reduced cognitive load rarely go tested. The third problem is that microservices only pay off when the organization changes alongside them—requiring cross-functional teams, decentralized decision-making, and a mature DevOps culture—and changing organizational structure is far harder than changing software architecture. Even Sam Newman advises that most organizations shouldn't adopt microservices without a compelling reason. Miell suggests starting from concrete problems like shortening release cycles, improving reliability, or removing bottlenecks, and only then asking whether microservices are the right solution.

## [Not all AI-assisted programming is vibe coding (but vibe coding rocks)](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Mar/19/vibe-coding/)

Simon Willison argues that the term "vibe coding," coined by Andrej Karpathy in February 2025, is being misused as a catch-all for any AI-assisted programming. By Karpathy's original definition, vibe coding means "forgetting that the code even exists": accepting every change generated by an LLM without reading it. Responsible AI-assisted programming, by contrast, still involves reviewing, testing, and understanding every implementation detail. Willison's golden rule: never commit code to a repository if you couldn't explain exactly what it does to someone else.

Even so, Willison is a fan of vibe coding. It lowers the barrier for beginners, helps them build useful tools on their own, and is a great way for experienced developers to build intuition for what LLMs can and cannot do. The key is restricting it to low-stakes, disposable projects—ideally inside a sandbox like Claude Artifacts—and avoiding anything involving security, privacy, or financial risk. He also hopes to see safer tools emerge so newcomers can experiment without causing harm.

## [Career advice in 2025.](https://lethain.com/career-advice-2025)

Will Larson surveys the 2025 tech job market and identifies four major shifts. First, managers who advanced between 2010 and 2020 through strong hiring and team morale now face new expectations: getting into the details, driving faster execution, and leading the transition to AI. Second, the wave of foundation models has invalidated many old playbooks; products now need to be designed around progressive validation with human-in-the-loop verification for critical data, while staying prepared for models either making massive leaps forward or plateauing right where they are.

Third, non-AI companies are struggling to raise funding, leading to fewer promotions and raises, while equity at AI startups is far from a guaranteed payout. Fourth, companies are pushing existing teams harder to find growth, turning once-comfortable roles into high-pressure ones. Today it is rare to find great colleagues, prestige, and learning opportunities all in the same job. Larson's advice: don't sit on the sidelines waiting for the market to recover—make your current work count, and remember that these headwinds are industry-wide, not a reflection of your personal ability.

## [Tips For Better Interactions](https://staysaasy.com/saas/2025/03/17/interactions.html)

Stay SaaSy shares six practical tips for more effective workplace meetings and interactions. When someone says you seem "frustrated," don't accept that framing—restate calmly that you are escalating an issue or need more information so you aren't dismissed as acting out of emotion. Volunteer to take notes in meetings: it signals humility, lets you help steer the discussion, gives you a natural pause to stay composed, and shows people their points are being heard. Avoid pushing back with extreme slippery-slope hypotheticals like "if we let everyone choose their own project, it'll be chaos," which implicitly insults the proposer's judgment and wastes time.

Don't interrupt someone mid-thought just to correct an immaterial detail; follow up privately afterward if needed. Schedule important meetings thoughtfully—avoid Mondays, Fridays, lunch hours, and the end of the day—and keep a consistent weekly slot for 1:1s. Finally, order meeting agendas by priority: thoroughly resolving two important topics is far better than rushing through five without reaching a conclusion.

## [Code is the new no-code](https://lumberjack.so/code-is-the-new-no-code/)

David Szabo-Stuban and Evan Boyle (CEO of GenSX) argue that the promise of the no-code movement is finally being realized in an unexpected way: through code itself. Visual programming tools are easy at first, but complexity balloons rapidly—a marketing automation workflow in n8n can easily sprawl into a spiderweb of 47 interconnected nodes. Abstractions inevitably leak, forcing users to learn technical concepts anyway, and power users usually end up migrating back to code.

According to the authors, AI coding assistants like Claude, ChatGPT, and GitHub Copilot have flipped the script: users describe their goal in plain language, the AI generates working code and explains each piece, and learning happens gradually instead of hitting a wall. Combined with familiar component models like React, code becomes even more intuitive than node-based visual editors. The post illustrates this with GenSX, where a workflow that fetches CRM data, filters it, and generates a report is written as readable JavaScript components, arguing that because most workflows are sequential, the tree structure of code is naturally easier to follow than a graph.

## [How to Improve JVM-Based Application Startup Time?](https://softwaremill.com/how-to-improve-jvm-based-application-startup-time/)

Michał Zyga (SoftwareMill) compares five approaches to shortening JVM application startup time, a critical metric for serverless and microservices. The JVM starts slowly because it has to load, verify, and initialize a large number of classes. Class Data Sharing (CDS) pre-packages core JDK classes and has been enabled by default since JDK 12; Application Class Data Sharing (AppCDS) extends this to application classes and became easier to use in JDK 19. GraalVM compiles ahead-of-time (AOT) into a native binary, CRaC snapshots a running JVM process using CRIU (Linux-only) for fast restore, and Project Leyden is a newer JDK effort that caches additional optimization data.

Benchmarks on a sample Netty application across two machines (a personal Intel i5 laptop and a Google Cloud Xeon instance, 50 runs per configuration) show that AppCDS more than doubles startup speed over the default, CRaC and GraalVM are the fastest overall, and Project Leyden sits in the middle while still under active development. Zyga recommends choosing based on context: AppCDS is an easy win with moderate gains, CRaC suits long-running services since it retains full runtime JIT optimizations, and GraalVM fits short-lived workloads that need near-instant startup.

## [Domain Driven Design](https://dev.to/lovestaco/domain-driven-design-3i2j)

Athreya (Maneshwar) introduces Domain-Driven Design (DDD), an approach to software design that puts the business domain at the center rather than focusing purely on technical concerns. The article walks through core DDD building blocks: domains and subdomains; Bounded Contexts that draw clear boundaries so models stay independent; Entities with distinct identities versus immutable Value Objects defined by their attributes; Aggregates that group related objects under an Aggregate Root; Domain Events that decouple system components; Repositories, Services, and Factories for persistence, domain operations, and object creation; and Ubiquitous Language, a shared vocabulary between developers and domain experts.

These concepts are illustrated through an e-commerce platform where order management is separated from the product catalog, accompanied by TypeScript examples of an `Order` class managing state and an `OrderRepository` handling persistence. The author notes that DDD shines in complex, long-lived projects requiring close collaboration between engineering and business teams, but can easily lead to over-engineering for simple CRUD apps; applying it well requires a solid grasp of design patterns and object-oriented principles.
